A definition of a System

A human cell – a system

  • 29,740 RNA genes; 148,792 gene transcripts; 21,257 protein-coding genes
  • Prediction of the features' behaviour is not reliable enough
  • All the mechanisms are not fully understood
  • Epigenetics
  • Can handle problems that computer scientists are struggling with today

Other examples of systems

Beating heart cells

Cardiomyocytes derived from induced pluripotent stem cells · Video: Cell Applications, Inc.

Heart cells grown from reprogrammed stem cells, beating in a dish. Each cell can contract on its own, but coupled neighbours beat together: the rhythm belongs to the population, not to any single cell or molecule. That leads into “More is different”.

More is different

“At each level of complexity entirely new properties appear.”

P. W. Anderson, “More is different”, Science 177:393 (1972)

Anderson: “Psychology is not applied biology, nor is biology applied chemistry.” Knowing the laws of the parts does not let you reconstruct the behaviour of the whole.

Rules of the Game of Life

Each cell looks at its 8 neighbours. All cells update at the same time.

The fate of the centre cell (outlined) in the next generation depends only on how many of its eight neighbours are alive now. A dead cell comes alive with exactly three live neighbours. A live cell survives with two or three, and dies with fewer (loneliness) or more (overcrowding). That is the whole rule set. Everything on the next slide follows from it.

Game of Life

Born with 3 neighbours
Survives with 2 or 3

Conway's Game of Life, in Gardner, “Mathematical Games”, Scientific American 223 (1970)

A grid of cells, each alive or dead. Every generation, a dead cell with exactly three live neighbours comes alive, and a live cell with two or three live neighbours survives; all others die. From a random start, still lifes, blinking oscillators and gliders that travel across the grid appear. None of these is in the rules. The glider gun emits a new glider every 30 generations. The Game of Life is even Turing complete: a universal computer can be built from it. Click or drag on the grid to add cells.

Flocking

Reynolds, “Flocks, herds and schools”, SIGGRAPH 1987

Each bird only sees its neighbours and follows three rules: separation (don't crowd), alignment (fly the way your neighbours fly), cohesion (move towards your neighbours). No bird knows the shape of the flock. Switch rules off one at a time: without alignment there is no common direction; without cohesion the flock dissolves; without separation it collapses into clumps.

The repressilator

Elowitz & Leibler, Nature 403:335 (2000)

Top: one gene repressing itself settles to a steady level. Bottom: three repressors in a ring (lacI ⊣ tetR ⊣ cI ⊣ lacI) oscillate, although no single gene oscillates. Built in E. coli with a GFP readout; the period (hours) is longer than the cell cycle. Simulated with the Elowitz & Leibler model (α = 216, α₀ = 0.216, β = 5, n = 2), same parameters in both panels.

Spontaneous oscillations in a communicating cell population

Each yeast cell senses and secretes acetaldehyde. Alone, a cell is quiet; above a critical cell density, the whole population oscillates.

Figure: Wang & Tang, “Emergence of collective oscillations in adaptive cells”, Nat Commun 10:5613 (2019), CC BY 4.0

Yeast glycolysis, one of the paper's examples. Each cell's glycolysis responds to acetaldehyde (via its NAD/NADH balance) and releases acetaldehyde into the medium, where it is diluted and degraded. The cells adapt: they respond to changes in the acetaldehyde level rather than to the level itself, so they react slightly ahead of the signal. That lead compensates the lag of the medium, and with enough cells the amplification beats the loss. Above a critical density the quiet state becomes unstable and the population oscillates together (dynamical quorum sensing). No cell oscillates on its own: the rhythm is a property of the population and its shared medium. Contrast with the repressilator, where the oscillation is wired inside one cell.

Can we read the cell's program?

Figure: Jonas & Kording, “Could a neuroscientist understand a microprocessor?”, PLoS Comput Biol 13:e1005268 (2017), CC BY 4.0 · Lazebnik, “Can a biologist fix a radio?”, Cancer Cell 2:179 (2002)

A computer comes with a wiring diagram and source code; a cell does not. Jonas and Kording took a classic microprocessor, the MOS 6502 from the Apple II and Atari, mapped every transistor from microscope images and simulated it running video games. They then analysed it with the data analysis tools neuroscientists use on the brain: lesions, tuning curves, correlations, dimension reduction. The tools gave plenty of results, but none revealed how the processor works. Lazebnik made the same point earlier with a radio. For a cell we have no wiring diagram to start from. We have to infer how it works from measurements, and that takes careful data analysis and models.

We observe the cell through measurements

Each layer of the cell from the earlier slide is measured with a high-throughput technique. Every experiment gives a matrix with far more variables (genes, proteins, metabolites) than samples. The values are noisy and strongly correlated, and the patterns we look for are hidden in them. Analysing such matrices is what the coming lectures are about. (The heatmap is an illustration, not real data.)

Walkthrough of Course PM

Aim of the course

After passing the course, you should be able to:

  1. Describe widely used statistical and computational techniques employed to investigate data from high-throughput techniques for measuring biopolymers.
  2. Explain the theory of state-of-the-art tools/algorithms for combining data from high-throughput molecular biology experiments.
  3. Choose appropriate machine learning strategies for processing molecular biology data.
  4. Apply network biology tools for interpreting data.
  5. Interpret the results of these analyses in a biologically or medically relevant context.
  6. Reflect over the choice of methods and tools and how it influences the outcome of the analyses.

The modules of the course

The course is organized in 4 modules:

  1. Machine Learning and Statistics – LK
  2. Network Biology and Metabolic Engineering – LK
  3. Multicellular transcriptional systems – SK

The LLM ate my homework …

26,811 pupils aged 12–18, followed for 30 months. Source: Strömberg, Lei & Wu, “The generative AI learning penalty: evidence from Chinese secondary education”, CEPR DP 21577 (2026). Redrawn from the paper’s Fig. 2.

Chinese secondary school pupils, staggered adoption of generative AI (difference-in-differences). Homework scores rise by 18% and completion time drops by 30%, but closed-book exam scores fall by 20% within six months. High-stakes entrance exams fall by 18–24%, with the full penalty after about two years.

Rightly used, there is nothing bad with an LLM

Ergo: All use of generative AI is prohibited in the course's examination and graded assignments.

26,811 pupils aged 12–18; median monthly exam score by homework completion time. Source: Strömberg, Lei & Wu, “The generative AI learning penalty: evidence from Chinese secondary education”, CEPR DP 21577 (2026). Redrawn from the paper’s Fig. 5.

The losses are concentrated among the roughly 80% of AI users whose behaviour looks like homework outsourcing: very short completion time and high homework scores. AI users who spend as long on their homework as non-users score the same on the exams. The penalty comes from skipping the work, not from the tool itself.

Use of Generative AI

All use of generative AI is prohibited in the course's examination and graded assignments, as it may negatively affect your ability to meet the course's learning objectives.

  • All examinations, computer exercises and graded assignments must be carried out without the help of generative AI.
  • All submissions must be completely human-generated.
  • This includes all debugging of code.

You are fully responsible for everything you submit and must be able to defend and explain it without the support of generative AI. Unauthorized use may result in disciplinary action.

Run the labs in Jupyter Notebook – editors such as VS Code and Colab have AI assistants built in.
